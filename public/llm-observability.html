LLM Compliance Observability

LLM observability tools tell you if the model is fast. BiasOps tells you if the model is legal. Six monitoring layers that track regulatory boundaries, fairness, provenance, PII lineage, hallucinated compliance claims, and disclosure requirements. All in real time.

Real-world scenario

Meridian Bank · LLM-Powered Loan Advisor

Meridian Bank deploys an LLM-powered advisor that helps customers understand loan options, check eligibility, and submit applications. The system uses RAG over internal product documents, customer financial records, and regulatory guidelines. It processes 15,000 conversations per day. Three months in, an internal audit discovers the advisor has been giving shorter, less detailed responses to applicants from certain ZIP codes, fabricating compliance certifications for products that haven't been reviewed, and leaking information between customer sessions through the shared vector store. None of their existing observability tools, which tracked latency, token cost, and general hallucination rates, caught none of it.

Why generic LLM observability misses the real risks

Every LLM observability tool on the market monitors the same plane: engineering metrics. Latency, token cost, hallucination rate, retrieval relevance, prompt quality. These are useful for debugging and cost optimization. They are completely blind to regulatory risk.

A bank does not get fined because latency was high. A healthcare company does not get sued because token costs spiked. They get fined because the LLM crossed a regulatory boundary no one was watching. It gave financial advice it was not authorized to give, treated demographic groups differently, fabricated a compliance claim, or leaked PII across tenant boundaries.

BiasOps operates on a fundamentally different monitoring plane: compliance observability. Not "is the LLM working well?" but "is the LLM operating within regulatory boundaries, in real time, with proof?"

The 6 monitoring layers

Layer 1 · Critical

Regulatory boundary detection

Catch the moment your LLM crosses a legal line
LLMs do not know where the legal boundary is. A customer service chatbot at a bank casually says "based on your profile, I'd recommend this mortgage rate". That is unlicensed financial advice (SEC, FINRA). A healthcare bot says "this looks like it could be melanoma". That is practicing medicine without a license. A hiring assistant says "your age might make this role challenging". That is age discrimination. Current observability tools do not flag any of this.

BiasOps classifies every LLM output into regulatory activity categories in real time: financial advice, medical diagnosis, legal counsel, insurance underwriting, employment decisions. When an output crosses into a regulated activity the system wasn't authorized for, BiasOps flags it instantly and can block the response before it reaches the user.

Financial advice boundary (SEC, FINRA, state regs) real-time
Medical diagnosis boundary (FDA, state medical boards) real-time
Legal counsel boundary (unauthorized practice of law) real-time
Credit decision language detection (ECOA, FCRA) real-time
Insurance recommendation boundary (state DOI) real-time
Why this does not exist yet: Langsmith tracks latency. Arize tracks hallucinations. No one tracks whether the LLM just committed a regulatory violation.
Layer 2 · High

Response fairness monitoring

Are different people getting different quality of service?
When different demographic groups interact with the same LLM, do they get the same quality of service? Research shows LLMs give shorter, less helpful, more dismissive responses to queries associated with certain demographics. In Meridian Bank's case, applicants from lower income ZIP codes received responses averaging 40% fewer words, with fewer product alternatives offered and more abrupt closing language. That is disparate treatment, and in regulated contexts, it is illegal.

BiasOps continuously measures response quality parity across demographic cohorts. It tracks response length, helpfulness score, tone sentiment, information density, and follow-up offer rates, segmented by user demographic signals. Statistical tests run automatically to detect disparate treatment before it becomes a pattern.

Response quality parity across demographic cohorts < 10% variance
Tone and sentiment consistency analysis continuous
Information completeness by user segment < 15% gap
Follow-up engagement parity continuous
Denial language disparity detection per-session
Why this does not exist yet: Traditional fairness tools measure model predictions (approve/deny). LLM fairness is about treatment quality: how the model talks to you, not just what it decides.
Layer 3 · Critical

Adverse decision tracing

When the LLM says no, prove exactly why
CFPB requires specific, individualized denial reasons for credit decisions. EU AI Act requires meaningful explanations for high-risk AI outputs. When an LLM-powered agent chain denies a loan, rejects an insurance claim, or flags an applicant: which step made the decision? What information did it have? Can you produce a legally defensible explanation? A Langsmith trace shows you the API calls. It does not produce an adverse action notice a regulator will accept.

BiasOps captures full decision provenance for every LLM-powered adverse action. It traces the entire agent chain: which retrieval context was used, which prompt produced the decision, which tool call triggered the outcome. It auto-generates regulation-specific adverse action notices with individualized reasons mapped to actual model inputs, not generic buckets.

Layer 4 · High

PII flow governance

Track where personal data goes inside your RAG pipeline
RAG pipelines pull documents from multiple sources: customer records, internal memos, policy documents. Is information from Customer A's file leaking into Customer B's response? Is PII being stored in vector databases without consent? Is the LLM memorizing sensitive data across sessions? Everyone scans outputs for SSNs. The real risk is cross-contamination inside the pipeline.

BiasOps tags PII at ingestion, tracks which vector store chunks contain sensitive data, monitors tenant isolation between customers, and verifies that output PII only originates from the requesting user's authorized data sources. It also detects cross-session memory contamination, where the LLM carries information from one conversation into another.

Cross-tenant data isolation in vector stores per-query
PII lineage from source document to LLM output per-response
Consent chain verification (authorized for this use?) per-retrieval
Cross-session memory contamination detection continuous
Right-to-deletion compliance (GDPR Art. 17, CCPA) on-demand
Why this does not exist yet: Everyone scans outputs for PII. No one tracks where the PII came from and whether the user consented to that specific use.
Layer 5 · Critical

Hallucinated compliance detection

When the LLM fabricates a compliance claim no one verified
This is the highest liability risk in LLM deployments. An LLM says "this product complies with Regulation Z" when no compliance check was ever run. Or "your application meets all Fair Housing requirements" when no audit has been performed. Hallucinated compliance claims are worse than no compliance. They create false confidence, legal liability, and evidence of negligence. Generic hallucination detection does not distinguish between a wrong fact about the weather and a fabricated regulatory certification.

BiasOps maintains a regulatory knowledge graph and cross-checks every compliance claim the LLM makes against actual policy status, audit results, and regulatory requirements. If the LLM says something is compliant, BiasOps verifies it actually is, or flags the hallucination as a critical compliance event with an auto-generated correction.

Regulatory claim verification against actual audit status per-response
Fabricated certification or approval detection per-response
False policy reference identification per-response
Unauthorized compliance assurance flagging per-response
Auto-generated correction with accurate regulatory status on-flag
Why this does not exist yet: Hallucination detectors ask "is this factually true?" BiasOps asks "did the LLM just make a compliance claim it cannot back up?" A fundamentally different and higher stakes question.
Layer 6 · Required

Transparency and disclosure compliance

The foundational layer auditors check first
EU AI Act requires disclosure when users interact with AI. NYC LL144 requires disclosure for AI in hiring. CFPB guidance requires disclosure when AI influences credit decisions. These requirements vary by jurisdiction, use case, and risk level. Is your system disclosing correctly in every context? Are the disclosures actually meaningful, or are they buried footnotes that do not meet the regulatory standard?

BiasOps maintains a jurisdiction-aware disclosure requirement matrix that covers which regulation, which jurisdiction, which use case, and what language. It verifies that every user interaction includes the correct disclosure, monitors for downstream systems that strip or hide disclosures, and ensures disclosures meet the "meaningful" standard required by regulators. Not just a checkbox footnote.

Jurisdiction-specific disclosure requirement matching per-session
Disclosure presence and placement verification per-response
Meaningful disclosure standard compliance per-session
Downstream disclosure stripping detection continuous
Multi-language disclosure accuracy per-locale
Why this does not exist yet: Pure regulatory plumbing. No one builds it because it is unglamorous. But it is the first thing an auditor checks.

The bottom line

Every LLM observability tool monitors the engineering plane: latency, cost, generic hallucinations. Useful for debugging. Completely blind to regulatory risk.

BiasOps monitors the compliance plane. Six layers that answer the questions regulators actually ask: Did the model cross a legal boundary? Did it treat people fairly? Can you explain the decision? Where did the data come from? Did it fabricate any compliance claims? Did it disclose what it is?

That's not observability. That's compliance as architecture, applied to the LLM layer.
